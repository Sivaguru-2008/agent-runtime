\section{Limitations and broader impact}\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{Human validation}
Independent human validation of the labels was designed, preregistered and fully instrumented but never collected. The only evidence that the constructed labels are shared by readers outside the authors comes from a panel of language models---a weaker control, because a notion of ``the same act'' shared by the authors and the models would pass it and could still diverge from human practice. The panel's preregistered re-derivation was itself defective (Section~\ref{sec:res-labels}). The instrument, protocol and analysis are provided so that the human study can be run exactly as preregistered.

\paragraph{Constructed labels and a single corpus}
Every label is an operationalisation of the authors' definition of identity. The safety conclusion was stable under the relabellings we could construct; the liveness conclusion was not. The corpus is synthetic and purpose-built, covers six tools, and says nothing about fleet-scale traffic, other tool surfaces, or the distribution of paraphrase in production. The empirical rates are properties of this corpus; what transfers are the decomposition, the bound and the dilemma.

\paragraph{Dependence on schema quality}
The certifier and the refuter are exactly as sound as the declarations they read (A5, A5$'$). A field mis-declared as an identifier that does not determine the act breaks certifier soundness; an act-determining field declared as free text moves its distinction to the judge. Neither is detected automatically.

\paragraph{Free text}
Where distinctions are carried only by prose, the mechanism is only as good as its judge---and under injection it is worse: prose-only injection succeeded 84.0\% of the time on the primary judge. The refuter's protection does not extend to free text.

\paragraph{Judge dependence and drift}
Deduplication, silent escape and robustness to injection all depend on the judge; two of the three judges share a model family, and a third vendor was never reached. Model versions behind an endpoint change without notice, violating exchangeability until the pool is recalibrated. Scoring at temperature 0 does not make an API deterministic.

\paragraph{Calibration validity}
Theorem~\ref{thm:bound} needs exchangeability between the pool and deployed comparisons. Our split is stratified at the pair level of one corpus, and removing seed-level sharing raised mean wrong suppression by a factor of about 1.5--2. The bound is marginal: 10.5\% of seed-level splits exceeded $\alpha$ on the primary judge. It says nothing under distribution shift, including injection.

\paragraph{Candidate generation and multiplicity}
The candidate set is every effect committed in the same run for the same tool. Retries under a new run identifier and the same act reached through a different tool are missed. On the corpus $m=1$; the multiplicity correction is supported by simulation and by one live experiment with a weak, saturated judge, and response attribution at $m\ge2$ (Section~\ref{sec:problem}) is not measured.

\paragraph{External semantics, storage and concurrency}
The mechanism does not provide external exactly-once execution; a crash inside the commit window re-fires under paraphrase unless the provider supplies a key complete for identity, which no tool in the corpus has. The research log is not crash-atomic (A3 is only partially supplied), and concurrent dispatch (A7) was not tested.

\paragraph{Cost and missed deduplication}
The judge is consulted on 76.2\% of dispatches, at about 184 tokens per comparison in batches of ten (roughly 140,000 tokens per 1,000 dispatches) and about 2.1\,s per batched call for the primary judge; the reasoning judge costs about 2.8 times as many tokens. Stricter levels buy safety with liveness: at $\alpha=0.01$ the primary judge deduplicates 22.1\% and routes 64.8\% of duplicates to review.

\subsection{Ethics and broader impact}

The mechanism is meant to reduce harm from irreversible agent actions, and its two failure modes carry different ethical weight. Wrongly suppressing a legitimate action---a refund never issued, a message never sent---is silent, and the person affected may never learn of it; the design bounds this error but does not eliminate it, and the bound is marginal. Executing an unintended duplicate is visible and recoverable by reconciliation, and the design deliberately tolerates more of it. When calibration is insufficient, when the judge abstains, and inside the commit window, the fence defaults to execution rather than semantic suppression, flagging the request where it has grounds for suspicion.

Adversaries can target both directions. A crafted request can make a distinct act look like a duplicate (a malicious semantic collision) so that it is swallowed, or disguise a duplicate so that it fires twice. The refuter removes the first attack whenever the distinction is typed; where it is not, the attack succeeded in a large fraction of our trials---a reason to type act-determining fields rather than to trust a better judge. The injection templates in the corpus are standard in the prompt-injection literature and are released for defensive evaluation.

Human oversight enters through the escalation band and through audit. A band that emits more flagged actions than reviewers can handle is not a safety mechanism~\cite{turan2026oversight}, and a wrong suppression can be recovered only if suppressions are recorded and sampled for review; the fence records every semantic suppression with the matched effect, the score, $m$ and the threshold, and every pending intent and flag. We do not recommend using the mechanism as the only safeguard for financial or safety-critical agent operations. The corpus is synthetic and contains no personal data.
