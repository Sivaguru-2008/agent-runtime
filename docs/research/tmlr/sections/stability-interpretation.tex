The calibrated threshold does not move: the pool yields $\thi=\stabMFThr$ whether it is scored in the original run or the fresh one, so matching the calibration and deployment scoring contexts changes no decision (rows 1--2 against 3--4). What varies is the test side. Across three independent scorings of the same 144 distinct residual pairs---the original run, the fresh run and the multi-candidate run---residual wrong suppression is \stabMPWSCount, \stabMFWSCount{} and \stabMTWSCount{} (\stabMPWS, \stabMFWS{} and \stabMTWS), and deduplication moves with it (\stabMPDedup, \stabMFDedup{} and \stabMTDedup). The judge is therefore a stochastic oracle even at temperature 0, with its noise concentrated on the few pairs scored near the threshold. That noise is compatible with the bound---each interval contains $\alpha$, and a score that is re-drawn independently for pool and test pairs alike preserves exchangeability---but it means the realised rate of any single scoring is itself a draw, and the original run, on which our headline figures rest, lies at the favourable end of the three. A deployment should expect a residual rate near $\alpha$ rather than near the best observed value, and should monitor it over time.
